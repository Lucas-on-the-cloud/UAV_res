Presenter A 2 / 15

The Problem We Are Solving

When a person goes missing in a wilderness area — hikers lost in forests, mountaineers stranded on slopes, survivors in flooded or earthquake-hit regions — the search radius grows quadratically with elapsed time, and ground search teams cover terrain slowly.

UAVs can sweep that terrain orders of magnitude faster, but a human operator watching the live video stream cannot reliably spot a ~30-pixel person against forest, rocks, or vegetation across hours of footage.

Automated aerial person detection is the real SAR bottleneck: turn a UAV's camera feed into actionable detections in real time.

Technical task: given an aerial RGB image (~4000×3000 px) captured from a UAV at typical SAR altitude, detect every person with tight bounding boxes, in real time, on hardware that fits on a drone or ground station.
Presenter A 3 / 15

Three Core Constraints

1. Tiny persons

A typical HERIDAL person is ~30 px wide in a 4000×3000 image (< 0.1% of image area).

Detectors trained at imgsz=640 see this person shrunk to ~5 px — they detect it but cannot localize tightly.

2. Natural distractors

Aerial views over wilderness contain rocks, huts, ruins, and vegetation patches with person-like silhouettes and aspect ratios.

False positives on these distractors are the dominant remaining error mode after small-object accuracy is fixed.

3. Scarce labeled data

HERIDAL: 1,124 training images.

COCO ~118k, VisDrone ~10k — two orders of magnitude smaller.

Anything requiring lots of labelled SAR data is off the table.

These three constraints drive every design choice in this project.

Presenter A 4 / 15

Our Approach: Pipeline, Not a New Detector

This project builds a pipeline — not a new detector — for aerial SAR person detection. The base detector is YOLOv12-s (Tian et al., NeurIPS 2025) and is held constant across all phases.

Every improvement attaches to a clearly-scoped change outside the detector: training data composition, training-time preprocessing, inference-time slicing, or post-processing.

Common path

  • Design new architecture / attention modules
  • New loss
  • Risk: overfits in 1,124-image regime
  • Every Δ confounded with architectural advantage

Our path

  • YOLOv12-s, released weights, never modified
  • Change: training data, preprocessing, inference, post-processing
  • Every Δ attributable to one specific component
  • Pipeline transfers to any detector (YOLOv8/v11/RT-DETR)

Presenter B 8 / 15

The Hidden Problem: Train–Inference Scale Mismatch

What standard training does:

Image 4000×3000 → resize to 640×640 → train detector

30-px person shrinks to ~5 px in training input

Model never sees persons at their actual scale

The natural fix: SAHI sliced inference

Cut 4000×3000 into 640×640 tiles at native resolution

Persons appear at their real ~30-px size in each tile

Applying SAHI directly to Phase 2 model:

mAP@0.5: 0.759 → 0.255 ❌

AP_small ≈ 0.002 — model hallucinates hundreds of detections per tile.

A model trained on 5-px persons cannot recognize 30-px persons. Out-of-distribution input → catastrophic failure.

Presenter B 9 / 15

Plan A — Couple Training and Inference Scales ★

Move the density-aware step into training, so that train distribution = inference distribution.

1

Generate 640×640 crops

  • Per 4000×3000 image: 2 person-centered crops (jitter ±160 px) + 1 random crop
  • Native resolution, no resize
  • Keep GT box if >50% of its area falls in the crop
  • Total: ~4,500 train crops
2

Train YOLOv12-s on crops

  • Init from COCO-pretrained weights
  • 80 epochs, AdamW, lr=0.001
  • Batch 16, AMP, single T4
3

SAHI inference on val

  • Original 4000×3000 → 640×640 tiles, overlap 0.2
  • ~48 tiles per image, native resolution
  • NMS post-process, match threshold 0.5
Training distribution = Inference distribution. The model sees persons at the exact scale it will encounter at inference.

Plan A established a strong baseline: mAP@0.5 = 0.872.

Plan A's scale alignment is the LOAD-BEARING piece. Any post-hoc add-on that doesn't preserve scale-awareness breaks something — each by a different mechanism. Foundation models (SDXL, SAM2) do not transfer for free to aerial small-person settings; they need domain-specific, scale-aware adaptation.
Presenter C 15 / 15

Three Takeaways

  1. Train–inference distribution ALIGNMENT is the dominant lever in aerial small-object detection — more than architectural change. Plan A delivers +0.113 mAP@0.5 and +0.229 mAP@0.5:0.95 without touching the detector.
  2. SAHI works only when paired with native-resolution training. Naive SAHI alone: 0.759 → 0.255. The two halves are coupled.
  3. Foundation models (SDXL, SAM2) don't transfer for free here. Both need scale-aware adaptation we haven't built yet — future work: crop-then-synthesize + StyleGAN2-ADA for data, SAM2 as a filter (drop) rather than a refit (replace) operator.
